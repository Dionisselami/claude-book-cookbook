# Recipe 03 — Plan once, then hold the outline

**Fixes:** strong openings, sagging middles, chapters that exist to reach a word count.

## Do this

```text
plan_book(premise, chapters: 32, words: 50000)
```

You get a chapter-by-chapter outline where each chapter carries a directive **and** a corpus style
reference. Print it. Save it as `outline.md`. Re-read the file — not your memory of it — before every
chapter.

## The rules that make planning worth it

1. **Plan once.** Re-planning mid-draft is how a book acquires two different middles. If the outline is
   wrong, say so explicitly and fix it in the file; silent improvisation is the failure mode.
2. **Every chapter does one job.** If a chapter's directive cannot be stated in a sentence, it is two
   chapters or none.
3. **Know your midpoint.** Chapter 16 of 32 is where the story's question changes shape. Outlines that
   sag are almost always outlines with no change of question at the halfway mark.
4. **Budget words per chapter and hold the budget.** A 4,000-word chapter 3 turns a 50,000-word book
   into 140,000 words and a tired agent by chapter 20.
5. **Write the ending before chapter 10.** The ending is a promise the outline makes; drafts that do
   not know their ending keep adding threads they cannot close.

## Structure check, at plan time

| Position | Should contain |
|----------|----------------|
| Chapter 1 | The premise, and the thing the protagonist wants, in scene — not in summary |
| ~25% | The first irreversible change; the door they cannot walk back through |
| ~50% | The question shifts: winning is no longer the same as wanting |
| ~75% | The cost becomes explicit; allies get expensive |
| Final 2 | Payoff of the *first* chapter's promise, not the last chapter's cliffhanger |

## Done when

`outline.md` exists, the midpoint does something, and you can name the ending without inventing it.
